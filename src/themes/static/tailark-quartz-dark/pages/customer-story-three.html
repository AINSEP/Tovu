When Fintra was small, friction was manageable. A handful of engineers could coordinate in a single thread, ship a new page in an afternoon, and redesign entire sections of the marketing site over a weekend. Decisions were quick, and the cost of inconsistency was low.

But scale changes the gravity of everything. What used to be a small inconvenience becomes a recurring tax. A design mismatch becomes a support burden. A slow iteration cycle becomes dozens of delayed experiments. As Fintra grew — more countries, more products, more markets — these points of friction didn't simply add up; they multiplied.

Fintra's marketing site is one of its most critical surfaces. It must communicate trust to enterprises, clarity to developers, and simplicity to founders evaluating payment infrastructure. As the product suite expanded, the burden on the team responsible for building the site increased proportionally.

The hidden cost of design fragmentation

Before Onyx Pro, Fintra's website evolved through many iterations. Teams built pages using slightly different layouts, custom patterns, and individually maintained components. Over time, the system became a patchwork where no single team understood the whole. It worked — until it didn't.

A few recurring challenges emerged:

  • Different product teams built pages using their own micro-patterns, creating subtle inconsistencies.
  • Engineering spent significant time re-implementing variations of components that already existed.
  • The velocity of new product launches outpaced the team's ability to produce polished, high-converting pages efficiently.

Small delays compounded into slower cycles. A messy system creates friction, and friction, at scale, becomes drag.

A shift begins quietly

The change didn't start with a mandate. It began with one team experimenting with Onyx Pro, looking for a faster way to launch a feature page without reinventing layouts or re-engineering components from scratch. The fit was immediate. The code matched their existing stack. The patterns felt intentional. The constraints felt liberating rather than limiting.

A few engineers pulled in Onyx Pro blocks. Then another team used it for a campaign page. Soon, the pricing team used it for a redesign. The momentum built naturally, driven not by policy, but by utility.

Clarity at the speed of Fintra

Fintra's marketing team began shipping pages at a pace that matched the company's ambitions. Large product launches that once required weeks could now be assembled in days. Regional variants rolled out faster. Experiments happened more often.

The results internally were unmistakable:

  • Cross-team dependencies became easier to navigate because everyone worked within the same design grammar.
  • Page production accelerated, not by a small margin, but by a meaningful factor.
  • Quality increased because the system made the right choices obvious.

Fintra didn't adopt Onyx Pro because they needed more features. They adopted it because they needed fewer obstacles.
