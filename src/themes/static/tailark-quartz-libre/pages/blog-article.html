The role of blockchain in modern finance

Understand how blockchain is reshaping the financial landscape.

Priya Nandan · October 29, 2025

Blockchain didn't arrive in finance as a single product — it arrived as an argument about who gets to keep the books. A decade in, that argument has settled into something more practical: a small set of real use cases where a shared, tamper-evident ledger genuinely beats the alternative.

A shared ledger, not a single owner

Traditional financial infrastructure relies on each party keeping its own records and reconciling them later — a slow, error-prone process that touches everything from cross-border payments to securities settlement. A blockchain replaces that reconciliation step with a single, shared record that every participant can verify independently, without needing to trust a central authority to keep it honest.

That property matters most where multiple institutions already have to agree on the same facts: trade finance, syndicated loans, and multi-party settlement are the clearest examples.

Faster settlement, fewer intermediaries

Cross-border payments today often pass through several correspondent banks, each adding a delay and a fee. Blockchain-based rails can settle the same transfer in minutes rather than days, because there's no queue of intermediaries independently updating their own ledgers.

The same logic applies to securities: tokenized bonds and equities can settle same-day instead of the industry-standard two-day cycle, freeing up capital that would otherwise sit in transit.

Where the risk still lives

None of this removes risk — it relocates it. Smart contract bugs, custody failures, and regulatory uncertainty are now the primary sources of loss in blockchain-based finance, replacing (rather than eliminating) the operational risk of traditional back-office reconciliation.

Institutions moving into this space are, in practice, trading a well-understood set of risks for a less mature one — which is a reasonable trade only when the underlying use case genuinely needs a shared ledger.

What changes next

The next wave looks less like speculative tokens and more like plumbing: regulated stablecoins for settlement, tokenized treasury products, and permissioned ledgers shared between a bank and its known counterparties. Boring, in the best sense — infrastructure that works because nobody has to think about it.
